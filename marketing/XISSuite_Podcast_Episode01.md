## SEGMENT 1 — The problem nobody names (0:45 – 4:30)

**AMARA:** So Tunde, set the scene for me. Before any of this software existed — what does running a small retail shop actually look like?

**TUNDE:** It looks like juggling. Imagine you sell maybe eight hundred, a thousand different products. Baby items, groceries, whatever. Every single one has a price, a cost, a supplier, a shelf. And all of that lives... where?

**AMARA:** In her head.

**TUNDE:** In her head. And in a notebook. And in three different notebooks that don't agree with each other. So she's doing four jobs at once — cashier, stock manager, accountant, and customer service — usually all at the same time, usually while someone's waiting to pay.

**AMARA:** And where does it break? Because it clearly works well enough that millions of people run shops this way.

**TUNDE:** It breaks in the invisible places. That's the key thing. Three big ones. First — **stock leaks.** She doesn't know what she actually has. So she either runs out of the thing everyone wants, or she's got cash frozen in product gathering dust.

**AMARA:** Dead money on a shelf.

**TUNDE:** Dead money on a shelf. Second — **expiry.** If you sell anything perishable — food, cosmetics, medication — things quietly expire in the back. That's money you literally throw in the bin, and you never see it happen.

**AMARA:** Oof.

**TUNDE:** And third, the big one — **she can't see her own business.** At the end of the day she can tell you she was busy. She cannot tell you if she was *profitable.* Those are completely different things, and the gap between them is where small shops die.

**AMARA:** That's the thing that got me preparing for this episode. "Busy" and "profitable" feel like the same word when you're in it. They're not even close.

**TUNDE:** Not even close. You can have your best sales day ever and lose money, if you were discounting the wrong things or your fast-movers were out of stock and you sold the low-margin stuff instead. Without data, you're flying blind and *calling it* flying.

**AMARA:** So this is the soil XIS Suite grows in. It's not "here's a fancy app." It's "here's the specific pain of not being able to see your own shop."

**TUNDE:** Exactly. And what I love — and we'll get into this — is it wasn't built by someone who read about retail in a business school. It was built out of [INSERT: the real origin — e.g. "a family running an actual store and getting fed up"]. *(If you don't have a clean origin line, cut this sentence — don't fabricate one.)*

## SEGMENT 2 — What XIS Suite actually is (4:30 – 9:00)

**AMARA:** Okay. So somebody downloads this, or signs up — what *is* it? Give me the one-sentence version first.

**TUNDE:** One sentence: it's the operating system for a retail shop. The till, the stockroom, the accountant, and the marketing team — all in one place, all talking to each other.

**AMARA:** Let's break that into pieces, because "all in one place" is what everyone says. What makes it actually one thing instead of five apps in a trench coat?

**TUNDE:** (laughs) Great question. It's that they share the same brain. Watch — when a cashier rings up a sale on the **POS register**...

**AMARA:** The point-of-sale. The till screen.

**TUNDE:** The till screen. The moment that sale rings up, three things happen automatically. The stock count drops by one. The day's revenue goes up. And if that customer is a member, their loyalty points tick up. One tap. Three systems updated. No notebook.

**AMARA:** So the thing that used to be "sell now, reconcile later, maybe, if I have energy" —

**TUNDE:** — is just *done.* Instantly. That's the whole magic. It's not that any one feature is revolutionary. It's that they're wired together so the shop owner never has to reconcile anything by hand again.

**AMARA:** Okay, walk me through the big rooms in the house. Give me the tour.

**TUNDE:** So, first room — the **POS**. Fast, works on a tablet or a phone, handles cash, card, bank transfer, split payments. Built for someone ringing up a customer in five seconds flat.

**AMARA:** Because a slow till is a dead till.

**TUNDE:** A slow till is a line out the door. Second room — **inventory.** This is the heart of it. Every product, its cost, its price, its stock level. And it's smart about variants — so if you sell a shirt in small, medium, large, it tracks each size separately instead of lumping them into one blob.

**AMARA:** That sounds small but I imagine it's huge.

**TUNDE:** It's the difference between "I have 40 shirts" and "I have 40 shirts but zero mediums, which is the only size anyone wants." That detail is money.

**AMARA:** What's the third room?

**TUNDE:** **Reports and money.** This is where "busy versus profitable" gets answered. Sales reports, what's selling, what's not, what your margins actually are. And — this is a detail I really respect — the reports are bucketed by the shop's *own* local time. So "today's sales" means today where the shop actually is, not some server in another timezone cutting your day off at 1am.

**AMARA:** (laughs) That's one of those things you'd never think about until it ruins a report.

**TUNDE:** Until it ruins a report and you spend an hour thinking you lost money. Fourth room — and this surprised me — **marketing and customers.** Loyalty points to bring people back. Online orders, so the shop isn't trapped behind its own front door. And it even plugs into Facebook and Instagram ad tracking, so a tiny shop can run ads like a big brand and actually see what's working.

**AMARA:** Hold on — a corner shop running Meta ad tracking, the same tech the huge e-commerce brands use?

**TUNDE:** Same tech. That's the quiet radical thing here. Tools that used to belong only to companies with a marketing department — they're just *in the box* now.

## SEGMENT 3 — The clever bits (9:00 – 14:00)

**AMARA:** Let's talk about the parts that made you go "oh, that's clever." Because a lot of software is competent. What made you sit up?

**TUNDE:** Three things. First — **expiry tracking.** Remember the money-in-the-bin problem? The system knows the expiry date of your stock, and it warns you *before* things go bad. There's an "expiring soon" report and an alert right on the dashboard. So instead of discovering spoiled stock when you smell it —

**AMARA:** — you get a heads-up while you can still discount it and actually sell it.

**TUNDE:** Turn a total loss into a small win. That's the mindset of the whole product: catch the leak before it becomes a flood.

**AMARA:** What's the second clever bit?

**TUNDE:** **Search.** Sounds boring. It isn't. Most shop systems, if you spell the product name slightly wrong, you get nothing. XIS Suite has fuzzy *and* semantic search — meaning it understands typos, and it understands *meaning.* You search "baby soap" and it finds the product even if it's actually listed as "infant wash."

**AMARA:** So the cashier doesn't have to memorize exactly how every product was named two years ago.

**TUNDE:** Which, when you've got a thousand products and a line forming, is the difference between a five-second sale and a frustrated customer walking out.

**AMARA:** And the third?

**TUNDE:** The **AI image and barcode tools.** A small shop usually has ugly, inconsistent product photos — or none. There are built-in AI tools to clean up and generate product images, plus proper barcode-label printing. So a one-person shop can have a catalog that looks like a serious retailer's.

**AMARA:** There's a theme here. Every one of these is taking something that used to require a specialist — a photographer, a data analyst, a marketing agency — and folding it into the till.

**TUNDE:** That's the thesis of the whole product, honestly. *Give the small shop the superpowers of the big chain.* The big supermarket has a whole team watching expiry, analyzing sales, running loyalty programs, shooting product photos. XIS Suite is that team, compressed into software, priced for one person.

**AMARA:** And there's a multi-user angle too, right? It's not just the owner.

**TUNDE:** Right — this matters as a shop grows. You can have a cashier who only sees the till, and a separate **accountant role** who sees the books but can't mess with daily operations. Everyone gets exactly the access they need. So the owner can finally *delegate* without losing control — which is the thing that usually stops a small shop from ever becoming a medium-sized one.

**AMARA:** That's the growth unlock. You can't scale if you're the only person allowed to touch anything.

**TUNDE:** You become the bottleneck in your own success. This breaks that.

# PRODUCTION PLAN — script → published audio

## Step 1 — Lock the script (30 min)
- Fill every **[INSERT]** or delete the line. Non-negotiable before you record.
- Read it aloud once yourself. Anywhere you stumble, rewrite for the ear, not the eye.

## Step 2 — Generate the voices (the "podcast" part)
You have three realistic routes, cheapest/fastest first:

**Option A — Google NotebookLM (fastest, free, most "podcast-like")**
- Go to notebooklm.google.com → create a notebook → upload this script (or a plain summary of XIS Suite).
- Use **Audio Overview** → it generates a two-host conversational podcast automatically.
- Caveat: it improvises its own dialogue from your source — you get less control over exact wording, but it sounds remarkably natural. Best if you want "good and done today."

**Option B — ElevenLabs (best control + quality) — recommended for a brand asset**
- elevenlabs.io → **Studio / Projects**. Create two voices (one for AMARA, one for TUNDE).
- Paste the script; assign each speaker's lines to their voice. Export a single mixed track.
- Cost: a ~20-min episode is a few dollars of credits on a paid tier. This is the route if this is a real, repeatable marketing channel.

**Option C — Play.ht / Murf.ai** — similar to ElevenLabs, comparable quality; pick on price/voice preference.

> I can't generate the audio file myself — these tools do the text-to-speech. My job ends at a clean, speaker-tagged script, which is exactly what all three want as input.

## Step 3 — Music & polish
- Intro/outro sting + low background bed. Free sources: YouTube Audio Library, Pixabay Music (both license-clear for commercial use — confirm each track's license).
- Mix in a free DAW: **Audacity** (simple) or **CapCut / Descent** if you also want video.
- Target loudness: **-16 LUFS** for a stereo podcast (standard for Spotify/Apple). ElevenLabs/Audacity can normalize to this.

## Step 4 — Make a video version (optional but high-ROI)
- For social reach, pair the audio with your existing **`XISSuite_Demo.mp4`** footage and the screenshots already in your repo (`StoreImage.jpg`, `FeaturedCollections.jpg`, `BabyToys.png`, `barcode-label-preview.png`).
- Cut 2–3 **60-second vertical clips** (the cold open, the "busy vs profitable" bit, the expiry bit) for Instagram Reels / TikTok / YouTube Shorts — these outperform the full episode for discovery.

## Step 5 — Publish & distribute
- **Audio home:** a free host like Spotify for Podcasters (anchor) → auto-distributes to Spotify; submit the RSS feed to Apple Podcasts.
- **Owned channels:** embed the player on XISSuite.com, and email it to your existing user list.
- **Paid loop:** you already run Meta ad tracking — use the 60-sec clips as ad creative and let the Pixel tell you which hook converts. Nicely on-brand: the product markets itself with its own feature.

## Step 6 — Measure
- Track plays, clip view-through rate, and (via your Meta Pixel) sign-ups attributed to the clips. Keep the hook that converts; re-cut the rest.
